Body weight is one of the clearest risk factors for tailbone pain, but not in the way most people expect. Research shows that **both** a higher body mass index (BMI) **and** being very thin can lead to coccydynia, through two different mechanisms.

## Key points

- A BMI **over 27.4 in women** or **over 29.4 in men** is a recognised risk factor for coccydynia.
- In people with a higher BMI, the pelvis tilts back less when sitting, so the coccyx meets the seat end-on and can be **pushed backward (luxation)**.
- In very thin people, the pelvis tilts back a lot and there is little padding, so the coccyx lies almost flat and can **bend too far forward (hypermobility)**.
- Rapid weight loss may also play a part, though this is based on clinical experience rather than strong studies.

## What happens when you sit

When you sit down, your pelvis rolls backward. Your weight is meant to rest mainly on the two **sitting bones** (ischial tuberosities), with the coccyx taking a small share and bending slightly forward. How far the pelvis rolls, and how much soft padding lies under it, decides how much load reaches the coccyx and in which direction.

::anim bmi-pelvis

| Body type | How the pelvis tilts | What happens to the coccyx | Typical finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| Higher BMI | Tilts back only a little | Meets the seat almost end-on and is pushed backward | Backward slip of a joint (posterior luxation) |
| Healthy BMI | Moderate tilt | Bends forward slightly (5–25°) while the sitting bones take most of the weight | Normal movement |
| Low BMI or rapid weight loss | Tilts back a lot; little padding | Lies almost flat and is bent forward | Excessive bending (hypermobility) |
Table: How body weight changes the way the coccyx is loaded (after Maigne et al., 2000; Garg and Ahuja, 2021).

In Maigne's study of 208 patients, obesity was confirmed as a risk factor, and the types of coccyx damage differed between obese, normal-weight and thin people. In women whose pain started after childbirth, a BMI above 27 was linked to a higher rate of luxation (Maigne 2012).

## What this means for you

- **If you are carrying extra weight**, gradual weight loss can reduce the load on the coccyx. Lean slightly forward when sitting, and use a cut-out cushion so the tip doesn't meet the seat.
- **If you are very slim or have lost weight quickly**, avoid hard seats, add padding, and take frequent breaks from sitting.
- **Either way**, the right treatment depends on how *your* coccyx is moving. That is why we examine carefully before treating.
