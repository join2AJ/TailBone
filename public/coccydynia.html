Coccydynia (tailbone pain): symptoms, causes and self-check

Understanding coccydynia

A small bone that can take over your whole day.

The coccyx is the last few small vertebrae at the base of your spine. It carries part of your weight every time you sit and anchors several muscles, tendons and ligaments of the pelvic floor. When it's injured, inflamed or out of position, the pain is called coccydynia.

It often follows a fall onto the backside, childbirth, long hours on hard seats, or repetitive strain like cycling or rowing. Sometimes there's no clear cause. Either way, it is treatable, and it rarely needs surgery.

Medical facts

What doctors know about tailbone pain

A plain-language summary based on leading medical references. It is general information and not a substitute for an examination.

How common is it?

Tailbone pain is common. Women are affected around five times as often as men, partly because of pregnancy, childbirth and the shape of the pelvis. It is unusual in children.

Usual causes

  • A fall onto the backside, or another injury
  • Pregnancy and childbirth
  • Long hours sitting, especially on hard seats or while driving
  • Repetitive strain, such as cycling or rowing
  • Being well above or below a healthy weight; very flexible or very stiff joints
  • Sometimes no clear cause is found

Typical symptoms

  • Aching or sharp pain at the very base of the spine
  • Worse when sitting, leaning back, or getting up from a chair
  • Pain with bowel movements or during intercourse
  • Poor sleep and low mood when it lasts

How it is diagnosed

Mainly through your history and a careful examination of the coccyx and surrounding muscles. Imaging is used when needed: X-rays (sometimes taken sitting and standing) for fractures or an unstable coccyx, and MRI to look for inflammation or other causes.

Treatment options

  • Self-care: a coccyx cushion, good posture with lower-back support, heat or ice, avoiding long sitting, softer stools
  • Hands-on therapy: physiotherapy, pelvic-floor work and manipulation of the coccyx and its muscles
  • Injections: local anaesthetic with steroid, when other measures don't help
  • Surgery (removing part or all of the coccyx) is rare and kept for cases where everything else has failed

Outlook

Most people improve within weeks with the right care. Pain that lasts more than a few weeks, or keeps coming back, deserves a specialist assessment rather than "just living with it".

⚠️ See a doctor promptly if tailbone pain comes with:

  • Fever or feeling unwell
  • Numbness, tingling or weakness in the legs or around the buttocks
  • New problems controlling your bladder or bowels
  • A lump, swelling or wound near the tailbone
  • Unexplained weight loss, or a history of cancer
  • Severe pain after a major accident

For sudden numbness around the buttocks or loss of bladder or bowel control, go to an emergency department straight away.

While you wait for your visit

Five small changes that ease tailbone pain.

1

Lean slightly forward

Shifting your weight onto your thighs takes load off the coccyx.

2

Use a cut-out cushion

A wedge or U-shaped cushion leaves the tailbone hanging free.

3

Stand up every 30 min

Short breaks stop pressure and stiffness from building up.

4

Avoid very soft sofas

Sinking in rolls you back onto the coccyx. Firm, supportive seats are better.

5

Keep things moving

Fibre and water help prevent straining, which often aggravates the pain.
