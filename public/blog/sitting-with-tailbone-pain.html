Sitting with tailbone pain: 8 practical ways to ease it

Desk job, long drives or a hard classroom bench? Small changes to how and how long you sit can take a lot of pressure off a sore coccyx.

If your tailbone hurts, sitting is usually the hardest part of the day. When you sit upright or lean back, part of your weight rests on the coccyx, the small bone at the very bottom of the spine. When it is bruised, inflamed or moving abnormally, that pressure turns into pain, and getting up from a chair can bring a sharp jolt.

The good news is that how you sit makes a real difference. These are the changes our patients find most useful.

1. Lean slightly forward

Tilting your upper body a little forward shifts your weight onto your thigh bones and the "sitting bones" of your pelvis, and off the coccyx. Sitting on the front half of the chair with your feet flat on the floor makes this easier.

2. Use a cut-out cushion

A wedge or U-shaped cushion with a gap at the back lets the tailbone hang free instead of pressing on the seat. Round "doughnut" rings help some people but can still press on the coccyx, so a cushion with an open back is usually better. Take it with you: to the office, the car and restaurants.

3. Choose firm seats over soft sofas

It sounds backwards, but deep, soft sofas let you sink and roll back onto the tailbone. A firm, supportive chair with a cushion is kinder.

4. Get up every 30 minutes

Pressure and stiffness build the longer you sit. Set a reminder to stand, walk a few steps or do a gentle stretch every half hour. On long drives, plan short breaks.

5. Stand up the smart way

Shift forward to the edge of the seat, bring your feet back under you, and push up through your legs while keeping your back straight. This avoids rocking back onto the coccyx at the moment you rise.

6. Support your lower back

A small cushion or rolled towel behind the lower back keeps you upright and stops you slumping, which tips weight back onto the tailbone.

7. Heat or ice

For a few minutes at a time, a warm pack can relax tight muscles around the coccyx, and an ice pack wrapped in a cloth can calm a flare-up. Use whichever helps you more.

8. Keep your bowels comfortable

Straining often makes tailbone pain worse. Plenty of water, fruit, vegetables and fibre help. If bowel movements are painful, ask your doctor or pharmacist about a gentle stool softener.

When self-care isn't enough

Most tailbone pain settles with time and sensible habits. If yours hasn't improved after a few weeks, keeps coming back, or stops you working or sleeping, it is worth having the coccyx and surrounding muscles properly examined. A displaced or stiff coccyx, or tight pelvic-floor muscles, often needs hands-on treatment rather than more waiting.

See a doctor promptly if the pain comes with fever, numbness, leg weakness, or problems controlling your bladder or bowels.
