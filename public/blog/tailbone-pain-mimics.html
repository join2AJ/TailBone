When it isn't the coccyx: conditions that can mimic tailbone pain

Pilonidal cysts, nerve pain, cysts on the sacral nerves, stress fractures and, rarely, tumours can all feel like tailbone pain. How they differ, and why a careful examination comes first.

Most pain at the base of the spine really does come from the coccyx and the muscles around it. But a few other conditions can feel very similar, and some need quite different treatment. This is why every patient is examined carefully before treatment starts.

Key points

  • Pilonidal cysts sit in the skin over the tailbone, not in the bone. They often cause a tender lump, redness or discharge.
  • Pudendal nerve pain is burning or shooting pain in the genitals, perineum or back passage, typically worse sitting and better standing or on a toilet seat.
  • Tarlov cysts on the sacral nerves are usually harmless and found by chance, but large ones occasionally cause pain.
  • Sacral insufficiency fractures happen without a fall in people with weak bones, mostly older women.
  • Tumours near the sacrum and coccyx, such as chordoma, are rare. Constant pain, pain at night and weight loss are warning signs.

Pilonidal cysts

A pilonidal cyst forms when hair and skin debris get trapped under the skin in the cleft between the buttocks. It is a skin problem, not a bone problem, and is treated by a surgeon or dermatologist. It is more common in young adults and people who sit for long periods. If you see or feel a lump, redness or discharge, have it checked.

Pudendal neuralgia

The pudendal nerve supplies the genitals, the perineum (the area between the legs) and the anus. When it is irritated or compressed, it causes nerve-type pain (burning, electric, pins and needles or numbness) in those areas. Like coccydynia it is worse when sitting, but the pain is felt more at the front and in the middle of the pelvis than at the tailbone itself. Cycling, long sitting and childbirth are known triggers.

Tarlov cysts

These small sacs of spinal fluid on the nerve roots of the sacrum are common on MRI scans and usually cause no symptoms at all. Finding one doesn't mean it is causing your pain. Only occasionally, when a cyst is large and its symptoms match, is it thought to be the cause.

Sacral insufficiency fractures

In people with weak bones, the sacrum can crack under normal body weight, with no fall. It is easy to miss on plain X-rays; MRI or CT shows it. Anyone over about 60, especially women with osteoporosis, who develops sudden pain low in the back or buttocks should have this considered.

Endometriosis

In endometriosis, tissue like the lining of the womb grows elsewhere in the pelvis. Deep endometriosis behind the womb can occasionally cause pain felt low in the back or near the tailbone. The key clue is that the pain follows the menstrual cycle. This needs a gynaecologist's assessment.

Tumours: rare, but important

Growths in or in front of the sacrum and coccyx, such as chordoma, sacrococcygeal teratoma or a tailgut cyst, are rare. They are mentioned here because they can be missed if tailbone pain is never examined. The warning signs are listed in our article on red flags in tailbone pain.

Why examination comes first

Coccydynia has a typical pattern, and a careful history and hands-on examination usually confirm it. When the story or the examination doesn't fit, or a red flag is present, we refer you for the right test or specialist before any treatment. Scans are requested only when they will change what we do.
