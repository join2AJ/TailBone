Coccydynia in numbers: what the research really says

How common is tailbone pain, who gets it, what causes it and how often treatment works: the key figures from medical research, each with its source.

By Tailbone Treatment Clinic · 17 October 2026 · 7 min read

  2. Why the exact numbers are uncertain

  4. High rates in particular groups
  5. How well does treatment work?

Coccydynia (tailbone or coccyx pain) is one of the most under-studied causes of back pain. This page brings together the numbers that are actually backed by published research, explains what they mean, and is honest about what is still unknown.

Key points

  • Nobody knows exactly how many people have coccydynia. It is thought to make up only around 1% of people seen for spine pain, but it is often missed or recorded as "low back pain".
  • Women are affected about five times as often as men.
  • Half or more of patients remember an injury such as a fall. Childbirth accounts for a smaller but important share in women.
  • Body weight matters at both ends: both a high and a very low body mass index (BMI) raise the risk.
  • About 9 in 10 people improve without surgery.

Why the exact numbers are uncertain

Coccydynia rarely gets its own diagnosis code. In many records it is grouped with general low back pain, and many people never see a doctor about it, often out of embarrassment. That is why the reviews we rely on say plainly that its true incidence is unknown. Be wary of any website quoting a precise share of the population: we could not find a reliable source for such figures.

Who is more likely to get it?

  • Women, partly because of the shape of the pelvis and because of childbirth. See why women get tailbone pain more often.
  • People with a higher BMI, or who are very thin. See body weight and tailbone pain.
  • People who sit for long hours or ride over rough roads. See two-wheelers and tailbone pain.
  • People with some other conditions, such as ankylosing spondylitis or pelvic floor problems. See conditions linked to tailbone pain.

High rates in particular groups

Some studies have looked at specific groups of people. Their figures are much higher than the general rate, because these groups are exposed to particular risks. They should not be read as rates for a whole country.

We have not found a published study measuring coccydynia across the general population of India. The two Pakistani studies are the closest regional data, and their settings (long sitting, motorbike commuting) are very familiar in Indian cities.

How well does treatment work?

Most people get better with simple measures: a cut-out cushion, changes to how and how long they sit, and hands-on treatment of the coccyx and pelvic floor muscles. Injections are an option if pain persists, and surgery is kept for a small, carefully selected group. See coccydynia treatment, step by step.
