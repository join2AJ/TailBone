Why women get tailbone pain five times more often than men

Women are about five times more likely than men to develop coccydynia. Pelvis shape, childbirth and pregnancy hormones all play a part. Here is what the research shows.

By Tailbone Treatment Clinic · 17 October 2026 · 6 min read

  1. Reason 1: the shape of the pelvis
  2. Reason 2: childbirth
  3. Reason 3: pregnancy and pelvic girdle pain
  4. Why diagnosis is often late

One of the most consistent findings about coccydynia is that it affects women far more often than men: about five women for every man. The reasons are a mix of anatomy, pregnancy and childbirth.

Key points

  • Women are about 5 times more likely than men to develop coccydynia.
  • A woman's pelvis is wider and differently angled, so the coccyx is more exposed when sitting.
  • Childbirth can push the coccyx backward. In one specialist clinic it accounted for 7.3% of women's coccydynia cases, and forceps had been used in about half of those deliveries.
  • Pelvic girdle pain affects about 1 in 5 pregnant women and can overlap with tailbone pain.
  • Pain after childbirth is often dismissed as "normal". If it lasts beyond a few weeks, it deserves an assessment.

Reason 1: the shape of the pelvis

A woman's pelvis is wider and shallower, which is needed for childbirth. When she sits, the coccyx sits further back relative to the sitting bones and is more likely to share the load. Reviews also point to greater ligament laxity and differences in coccyx shape. In men, the narrower pelvis tends to keep the coccyx clear of the seat.

Reason 2: childbirth

During a vaginal delivery, the baby's head pushes the coccyx backward. Usually it springs back, but a difficult or assisted delivery can strain the ligaments or push a joint out of place.

Reason 3: pregnancy and pelvic girdle pain

During pregnancy, hormones loosen the ligaments of the pelvis. Pelvic girdle pain (pain at the back of the pelvis or over the pubic bone) affects about 20% of pregnant women at any one time. It is a separate condition, but it can sit alongside tailbone pain and makes sitting and walking harder.

A Norwegian study of a multi-ethnic group of women found that women of South Asian and Middle Eastern origin reported 10–20% more pelvic girdle pain than Western women at every stage of pregnancy and after birth. The difference was not significant once other factors were taken into account, so it may reflect circumstances such as the number of children rather than ethnicity itself.

Why diagnosis is often late

Many women assume tailbone pain after delivery is part of recovery, or feel embarrassed to mention it. But pain that stops you sitting to feed your baby, or that is still there after a few weeks, is worth having examined. Hands-on treatment for a displaced or stiff coccyx is gentle and usually needs only a few visits. A female clinician is available at our clinic if you prefer.
