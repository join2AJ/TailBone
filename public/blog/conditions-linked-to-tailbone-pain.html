Conditions linked to tailbone pain: spondylitis, pelvic floor problems and more

Tailbone pain is more common in people with ankylosing spondylitis, pelvic floor dysfunction, back problems and fibromyalgia. What the studies show, and why it changes treatment.

By Tailbone Treatment Clinic · 17 October 2026 · 7 min read

Share on WhatsApp

On this page

  1. Ankylosing spondylitis and axial spondyloarthritis
  2. Pelvic floor dysfunction
  3. Low back and sacroiliac problems
  4. Fibromyalgia and a sensitised nervous system
  5. Inflammatory arthritis

Tailbone pain doesn't always start with a fall or a hard chair. Some people who live with other conditions are more likely to develop it, and knowing about these links helps get the right treatment.

Key points

  • Ankylosing spondylitis (AS): in one study, 38.3% of people with AS had tailbone pain, about three times the rate in people with ordinary back pain.
  • Pelvic floor problems: half of women seeking physiotherapy for pelvic pain also had coccydynia, and nearly 8 in 10 of them had a tight coccygeus muscle.
  • Fibromyalgia was about five times more common in women with coccydynia in the same study.
  • Low back problems often occur alongside tailbone pain, so both areas should be examined.
  • Rheumatoid and other inflammatory arthritis can affect the joints of the coccyx, which in some people have a true joint lining.

Ankylosing spondylitis and axial spondyloarthritis

Ankylosing spondylitis is an inflammatory disease that mainly affects the spine and the sacroiliac joints. Its typical pain is worse in the morning or after rest and eases with movement.

Why it matters: if tailbone pain started without an injury, is worse in the morning, and comes with back stiffness, it is worth checking for inflammatory disease. That needs a rheumatologist's treatment as well as local care.

Pelvic floor dysfunction

The coccyx anchors several pelvic floor muscles. When these muscles stay tight or spasm, they pull on the coccyx, and a painful coccyx in turn makes them guard more. This "pain–spasm–pain" cycle is common.

Why it matters: treating only the bone, or only the muscles, often isn't enough. Hands-on treatment that releases the pelvic floor muscles and restores how the coccyx moves addresses both sides of the cycle.

Low back and sacroiliac problems

Pain from the lower spine or the sacroiliac joints can spread to the area around the tailbone. People with coccydynia often have back problems too. A careful examination separates pain that truly starts at the coccyx (you can usually point to it with one finger) from pain referred from higher up.

Fibromyalgia and a sensitised nervous system

In fibromyalgia and related conditions, the nervous system amplifies pain signals. Normal sitting pressure can then feel very painful even when the coccyx itself is not badly damaged. The MSD Manual notes that coccydynia is occasionally part of such a generalised pain condition. Treatment then works best when it combines gentle local care with sleep, activity and pain-management support.

Inflammatory arthritis

In some people, the joint between the sacrum and coccyx, or between the first two coccyx segments, has a true joint lining. Diseases that inflame joint linings, such as rheumatoid arthritis, can therefore reach the coccyx. The MSD Manual lists arthropathies among the less common, non-injury causes of coccyx pain.
